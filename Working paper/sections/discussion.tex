\section{Discussion and policy implications}
Counting payments tells us what does and does not get used, but leaves out who does the work and who can act when a payment fails. The migrant synthesis and institutional sources show why those omissions matter. Someone can use a wallet for local sales and still depend on a contact to deliver money abroad. Likewise, a formally designated complaint channel may cover only one leg of the transfer. The joint display (Table~\ref{tab:joint-evidence}) sets out where these observations refine the interpretation of the survey and where the evidence remains insufficient.

The adjusted survey associations concern two reported own-name onboarding conditions and a separate score combining transfer ability and fraud-avoidance confidence. The two focal coefficient-null tests that survive Holm adjustment involve different outcomes; that is not a test that the associations differ across outcomes. Both highlighted contrasts have limited exact endpoint support. The migrant and expert accounts help identify distinctions these measures miss, such as holding proof of address versus having a provider accept it, or being able to make a payment with help versus controlling the account.

Three practical tests follow. First, can customers open and continue using an account under their own name when an accepted document changes, and get help when verification fails? Second, can a user see the total price and the amount due at destination before confirming a transfer? Third, if a transfer spans providers or an intermediary, can the user identify who handles the complaint, trace the payment and obtain a remedy? These tests separate usable access, transparent pricing and recourse from the number or speed of transactions. None presumes one universal barrier or treats cash use as failure.

The welfare implications concern things worth measuring: realized fees and exchange-rate spreads, travel or waiting time, delivery, losses and recovery, privacy, and control over spending. Some of these issues appear in the authored accounts; the survey's binary use outcomes cannot measure gains in them. Estimating welfare changes or ranking reforms would require outcomes and comparisons the present study does not provide. The contribution is to show why these questions should be asked at different stages of a payment.
