\subsection{What frequent use does and does not show}

The survey shows that domestic digital payments were common in the recruited sample. It also shows that fewer respondents reported using or keeping some proceeds of incoming remittances digitally. These outcomes answer different questions; their difference is not a measure of how often a transaction leaves the digital system. Accounts of sending money to Venezuela show how domestic use can coexist with reliance on someone to arrange conversion or delivery abroad. Accounts of more direct incoming transfers show why that arrangement cannot stand in for every remittance. Nor does the regional expert memo's concern about active wallet use contradict the survey result: it concerns a different measure, without a comparable denominator.

The adjusted associations point to conditions worth examining, not interventions we can rank. Reporting both own-name onboarding conditions rather than one is positively associated with digital retention or use of incoming proceeds. A higher score combining reported transfer ability and confidence in avoiding fraud is positively associated with domestic use. Both contrasts have limited exact endpoint support. The Holm-adjusted coefficient tests in Section~\ref{sec:survey-results} do not test differences between outcomes or resolve the limited support. Holding proof of address does not establish that a provider accepted it. Some women describe learning through use or transacting with help, so the ability association does not tell us whether prior skill leads to use, whether use builds skill, or what training would change.

The four observed onboarding profiles show which of two reported conditions are present together (Figure~\ref{fig:onboarding-conditions}). They do not establish who holds or controls the account. Likewise, the qualitative positions describe arrangements that may change with the task, not fixed groups of women. A relative's account can make a payment possible and still leave the person relying on it unable to act without help. Conversely, an account in one's own name does not establish control over household spending or access to the information needed for a decision. In the separate complete-item tabulation, 244 of the 360 respondents who agreed that they usually decide how household remittances are used reported very little or no information about fees, exchange rates and what to do if something goes wrong. Reported authority and access to information can come apart, just as access to an account and the ability to use it on one's own terms can \citep{kabeer1999}.

\subsection{Policy implications}

\paragraph{Make account access usable and durable.} The migrant and institutional accounts identify different problems: not having a requested document, having one that is reportedly rejected, and losing usable access when a document or phone number changes. Providers can test clearer instructions about accepted credentials, a way to resolve failed verification and a recovery process when documents or numbers change. Testing these steps on the devices and connections customers actually use would help distinguish a difficult process from a need for assistance. Offer help that lets users keep control of their credentials. Track completed, failed and abandoned attempts and access after a change, rather than counting registrations alone. The survey's two reported conditions should not be used as an eligibility rule or the basis for assigning users to services.

\paragraph{Measure the full transfer, not the fastest step.} Domestic system designs offer choices about how to connect existing services and who runs common functions. Peru's initial wallet linkage and Costa Rica's phone alias tied to an account illustrate different choices, not differences in realized inclusion \citep{bcrp2025,bccrsinpe}. For a cross-border transfer, the relevant comparison includes the exchange rate and total charge, the amount and form in which funds reach the recipient, and when those funds become usable. Providers handling a corridor could test quotes that state what the recipient should receive and confirmations that distinguish payment to a local account from delivery abroad. Informal help can make delivery possible when a direct route is unavailable or unattractive. A domestic transfer made in seconds is not evidence that a cross-border alternative is cheaper or more reliable.

\paragraph{Give users a way to trace a problem.} Checking a message or asking family to confirm arrival can help verify a payment, but does not identify who must respond to a dispute. Banco de la Rep\'ublica's documented complaint route for domestic Bre-B payments runs through the participating financial institutions \citep{banrepfaq}. When different actors handle payment, conversion and delivery, a clear handoff could tell users whom to contact and what to provide without implying that the domestic scheme covers every leg. For evaluation, record acknowledgement, resolution and any recovery separately, including whether the user recovered part or all of the funds. In our survey the complaint question follows a reported problem in the preceding 12 months, and first response and resolution are asked of those who complained. Those answers cannot establish the frequency of verified fraud or the share of all affected women who recovered funds.

\subsection{Welfare implications and limits}

The welfare question is whether people can complete the payments they need on better terms and with more control. Fees and exchange-rate spreads matter, but so do trips to withdraw cash, time spent coordinating a transfer, uncertainty about when money will be usable, and the consequences of a failure. Timing may matter for a household bill or a purchase for a small business even when the funds eventually arrive. Keeping proceeds digital can be useful when the balance can pay for what the recipient needs. Cash or help from a trusted contact can also be the better option when the digital route is costly or unreliable. These trade-offs explain why a rise in transaction counts is not, on its own, a welfare gain.

The study records experiences that make these costs and choices visible. It does not estimate changes in income, consumption, privacy, control over spending or financial loss, or the net benefits of any reform. The cross-sectional survey uses referral recruitment and different items have different valid-answer bases. It does not establish which rail carried a particular transaction. The interviews can show how a constraint arises without identifying how common it is; benchmark documents establish formal provisions without measuring who can exercise them. A future evaluation would need to follow actual attempts from initiation to usable delivery or failure, capture abandoned attempts, and observe what households or businesses could do with the funds. To estimate a welfare effect it would also need a credible comparison with the alternatives those users could access.
