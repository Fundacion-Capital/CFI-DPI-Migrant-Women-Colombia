\subsection{What frequent use does and does not show}

The first result is that using digital payments was common in the recruited sample. Keeping or using some incoming remittance proceeds digitally was less common on its own response base. The distinction matters: a domestic transfer can begin a journey that still requires conversion or delivery abroad, and the two survey questions neither follow one transfer nor track the same money. The more direct incoming arrangements in the migrant synthesis show why an outward example cannot stand for every remittance route.

The models identify two associations worth examining. The remittance comparison changes the reported onboarding score from one condition to two; the domestic-use comparison changes a score that combines reported transfer ability and fraud-avoidance confidence. As Section~\ref{sec:survey-results} explains, each average includes a stratum with an endpoint outside its observed range. The estimates do not verify that a provider accepted the proof of address or rank documentation and training as interventions. Use can build confidence as well as follow from it. Help can make a payment possible before the user can complete it alone.

The observed profiles show what combining many answers into one measure would hide. A phone line and proof of address do not always come together, and neither tells us who has the account credentials. Decision-making and information also differ. On the separate 407-case base, 244 of the 360 respondents who agreed that they usually decide how household remittances are used report very little or no information about fees, exchange rates and what to do if something goes wrong. The result is a reason to keep information and reported discretion separate, not evidence of how money was actually allocated \citep{kabeer1999}. Borrowed access and task-specific assistance add a further distinction between being able to act and deciding how to act.

\subsection{Policy implications}

\paragraph{Make account access durable.} A woman who lacks a requested document faces a different problem from one whose document was reportedly rejected. Both differ from losing usable access after changing a document or phone number. Providers can test whether clearer instructions, assistance that does not require sharing credentials, or a way to recover access address those problems. An evaluation should record completed, failed and abandoned attempts and check whether users retain access after a change. Neither the reported conditions nor fitted profiles should be used to screen clients.

\paragraph{Measure the full transfer.} Connecting services can help with the domestic step. Peru's initial wallet linkage and Costa Rica's account-linked phone service illustrate different choices about how to do that \citep{bcrp2025,bccrsinpe}. For an international transfer, the relevant price includes the exchange rate and fees, and the relevant time ends when the recipient can use the money. Providers could test quotes stating what the recipient should receive and notices that distinguish payment into a local account from delivery abroad. A trusted intermediary can make an otherwise difficult route work; the interviews do not rank its cost or reliability against a direct route.

\paragraph{Tell users who can act after a failure.} A local transfer, conversion and foreign delivery can involve different actors. The domestic Bre-B complaint channel does not automatically cover the later steps. A test of a clearer handoff would ask whether users know whom to contact and whether they get a response, a resolution or their funds back. The survey asks about first response and resolution only of respondents on the complaint route. It does not estimate the rate of verified fraud or recovery among everyone affected.

\subsection{Welfare implications and limits}

The welfare question is whether women can complete the payments they need on better terms and with choices they value. Fees and exchange-rate spreads matter, but so do trips to withdraw cash, time spent coordinating a transfer, and uncertainty about when money will arrive. Waiting can disrupt a household payment or a business purchase even if delivery eventually occurs. A digital balance is useful when it can pay for what the recipient needs. Cash or assistance may be preferable when the available digital route costs more or is less reliable. More digital transactions do not by themselves imply gains in welfare.

The study documents experiences that make those costs and choices visible. It does not estimate a change in income, consumption, privacy, control over spending or financial loss. To measure a welfare effect, an evaluation would have to follow attempted and abandoned transfers, observe what users could do with the funds and compare the outcomes with the alternatives they could actually use. The referral survey and the institutional design comparison do not provide that counterfactual.
