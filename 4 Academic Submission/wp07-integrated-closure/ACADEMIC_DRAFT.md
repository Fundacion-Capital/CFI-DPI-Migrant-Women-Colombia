# Digital payments after remittance receipt: evidence from migrant women in Colombia

*Working manuscript text for author review. Uses the project evidence traced
to the selected full legacy draft (31 July 2026), the accepted population and
measurement decisions, and the held academic code. This draft adds no
empirical estimates or qualitative claims. It is not the submission version.*

## Introduction

A remittance received through a digital channel can still end in cash. A
recipient may also make digital payments for everyday expenses even if she
receives remittances in cash. These distinctions matter for financial inclusion:
the channel through which funds arrive, the decision to keep or use them
digitally, and the ability to transact within the destination country answer
different questions. A measure of account ownership or access to a payment
service cannot tell us which of these steps people take or what constraints they
encounter once they try to use a service.

We study these distinctions among adult women with international migration
experience in Colombia whose households reported receiving remittances. The
survey measures how respondents generally handle remittance proceeds and
whether they report any domestic digital payment or transfer in the preceding
60 days. It also records experiences that can make a digital transaction
practical or costly: clarity about fees and exchange rates, access to cash-out,
reported problems, options for seeking help, and the arrangements through
which women manage money or make decisions about an account. We distinguish
these experiences rather than combine them into a single measure of financial
inclusion, security, empowerment, or well-being.

The paper complements research on financial inclusion and migration in
Colombia. Barboni et al. (2025) use an information experiment to examine
perceived social acceptance and Venezuelan migrants' willingness to engage
with financial providers. Martin-Shields et al. (2022) distinguish access to
digital technology from the use of public digital services in Bogotá. We ask a
different question: how do women in remittance-receiving households report
using money after it arrives, and how does this relate to their reported use
of domestic digital payments? Following Kabeer's (1999) distinction between
resources, agency, and achievements, we also distinguish a way to access or
move money from the ability to decide how it is used. The contribution is to
measure these stages separately and interpret them together, not to estimate
the effect of making a payment system available.

## Colombia's payments setting

Colombia's Bre-B interoperable instant-payment system entered full operation
on 6 October 2025 (Banco de la República, 2026), before the first recorded
interviews in this study. It connects participating domestic payment
providers; the central bank documents that instant transfers and private
initiatives already existed during the transition. The fieldwork therefore
describes behavior in an early period of full operation, not a move from a
world without digital payments to a world with them. A reported domestic
digital transaction does not identify its payment rail. Likewise, the
channel through which an international remittance arrived does not establish
that it settled over Bre-B or that it was subsequently spent digitally. The
survey is not a before-and-after design and provides no estimate of Bre-B's
effect.

## Data, measurement, and empirical approach

The recorded interviews span 9 December 2025 to 17 February 2026. Trained
migrant women leaders recruited through contacts and referrals. The intended
population included adult women who had migrated internationally, including
Colombian return migrants, with an intended residence period of about three
months after arrival or return. The programmed screening used consent, gender,
age, and remittance receipt by the respondent or someone in her household over
the preceding 12 months; it did not implement every intended recruitment
condition. The historical manual and coded exclusions define the retained
423-record cohort. This is a nonprobability sample. The referral structure and
household links were not systematically recorded, and the retained cohort
does not identify a population response rate or establish national
representativeness. Recorded residence is distinct from interview location.

The first outcome is whether any remittance proceeds are generally retained
or used digitally, as opposed to all being converted to cash. The five
original response categories remain visible in the descriptive results; they
are not monetary shares. The latest receipt channel is a separate categorical
measure. The second outcome is any reported domestic digital payment or
transfer in the past 60 days. Frequency is reported separately among
affirmative users, in the original bins, with uncertainty and other
unscored states distinguished. The two outcomes have separate valid-response
denominators and are not successive steps observed for a single transfer.

The quantitative analysis first describes the admitted cohort, records
availability and routing by instrument version, and then reports the two
outcomes on their own valid-response bases. Related domains use their
question-specific denominators. A question that was not offered, a question
that was not applicable, a refusal, and a substantive negative answer are
not interchangeable. The analysis distinguishes reported costs and problems
from outcomes of a complaint, which are only defined for the relevant
routed group.

The working regression specifications relate each binary outcome to selected
measures of access, perceived digital confidence, and formal financial access,
with age, grouped education, and version-aware grouped residence. The extended
specifications add occupation and first arrival calendar year; the year is
not a measure of current tenure. The common-base sensitivity addresses
differences in the sample available for the two core models, not confounding.
The results display fixed within-model probability contrasts with pointwise
uncertainty, accompanied by actual estimation denominators and support
diagnostics. These comparisons are conditional associations, not effects
of changing a person's access, skills, or formal financial arrangements.
Inference under the working model does not account for unknown referral or
household dependence.

## Interpreting outcomes for policy

The policy question is not simply whether women make more digital payments.
If respondents report unexpected charges, uncertain cash-out costs, or
difficulty recovering funds, an increase in transaction use could coexist
with costly experiences. Reporting fee clarity and recourse on their own
bases can help identify where providers or regulators might investigate
disclosure or complaint handling. Similarly, reported control over account
decisions is informative about household arrangements but is not evidence
that moving money digitally expanded women's bargaining power.

Lower travel time, improved access to funds in an emergency, reduced losses,
and greater control over spending are possible welfare channels, not effects
identified by this study. Recommendations should distinguish a friction
observed in the survey from a proposed response to that friction, and both
from the welfare effect of implementing that response. Existing qualitative
findings can explain how respondents experienced these choices; they should
not be used to extrapolate their prevalence. Expert and institutional
evidence can clarify which actors could address a problem. Regional
benchmarking can compare documented design and safeguards, not rank country
welfare gains from these data.
