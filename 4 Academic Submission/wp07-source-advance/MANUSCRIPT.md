# Digital payments after remittance receipt: evidence from migrant women in Colombia

*Working article sections for author review. This draft advances the
question, context, empirical approach and discussion from the selected
31 July 2026 legacy paper and the prior academic draft. It does not
insert new study results, quotations or release statements.*

## 1. Introduction

Receiving money digitally does not mean keeping it digitally. A woman
can receive a remittance in a wallet and withdraw it immediately. She
can also receive cash and make domestic digital payments from other
funds. If access to a service, the channel by which money arrives, and
what happens to it afterwards are counted as the same outcome, it is
hard to tell which part of the payment process a policy could improve.

We study this distinction among women with international migration
experience in urban and peri-urban Colombia. Our survey separates the
reported channel of the latest international remittance, the general
disposition of remittance proceeds, and whether the respondent made or
received a domestic digital payment or transfer in the previous 60 days.
The last two measures are the primary quantitative outcomes. They
describe different behaviors and do not trace the same transfer. The
study also records practical conditions of use, including fees,
cash-out, perceived safety, complaint handling and decisions about
accounts. Those measures help interpret why access may not translate
into use or control; they are not a composite welfare score.

The paper adds evidence about what happens after access becomes possible.
In an information experiment among regularized Venezuelan migrants in
Colombia, [Barboni et al. (2025)](https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/soc/economics/research/centres/cage/manage/publications/wp745.2025.pdf)
study how beliefs about social acceptance shape willingness to engage
with financial providers. Our data do not test that intervention:
they describe what respondents report doing with remittance proceeds
and whether they report using domestic digital payments.
Research in Bogotá distinguishes access to digital technology from use
of digital government services ([Martin-Shields et al., 2022](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/poi3.280)).
Our question concerns financial transactions rather than government
services. It also separates the ability to move or store funds from the
ability to choose how to use them. That distinction follows the difference
between resources, agency and achievements in
[Kabeer (1999)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1467-7660.00125).
The survey describes reported use and arrangements for decision-making;
it does not show that digital payments expanded respondents' choices.

## 2. Colombia's payments setting

Colombia's interoperable instant-payment system, Bre-B, entered full
operation on 6 October 2025. Participating providers and private instant
payment initiatives were already operating during the transition
([Banco de la República, 2026](https://www.banrep.gov.co/es/blog/bre-b-breve-historia-sistema-interoperado-pagos-inmediatos)).
Our fieldwork began after the start of full operation. The launch date
places the interviews in context, but the survey does not identify
which payment rail processed a transaction. Nor does a reported
international receipt channel establish cross-border settlement through
Bre-B. We do not estimate the effect of the rollout. In particular, a
post-launch observation is not a measure of exposure to the system.

Interoperability addresses whether participating providers can exchange
a payment. It does not by itself establish that a customer can open an
account, use an interface without assistance, understand a charge, or
obtain redress. These questions concern different institutions and
moments in a transaction. We therefore distinguish payment-system
design from respondents' reported experience. The regional comparison
can help identify which arrangements are available elsewhere; it
cannot establish that importing an arrangement would improve outcomes
for the women in this study.

## 3. Data and measurement

The survey's recorded interviews span 9 December 2025 to 17 February
2026. Fundación Capital recruited migrant women leaders, who recruited
through contacts and referrals and administered the survey. Recruitment
was not restricted to former program participants. The intended
population included adult women with international migration
experience, including Colombian return migrants, with about three
months' residence after arrival or return. The form's programmed
screening used consent, gender, age, and whether the respondent or
someone in her household received remittances in the previous 12
months. It did not test every intended recruitment condition, and
household receipt did not establish that the respondent personally
received or managed the funds.

The quantitative revision retains the historical cohort of 423 survey
records and the original manual and coded exclusions. We do not treat
the cohort as a probability sample. The fieldwork did not systematically
record referral links or household links, and the record count is not
a count of verified distinct households. The data do not identify the
number approached or a response rate. The paper reports residence as
recorded, without treating it as the interview location or reassigning
unverified locations. Table 1 describes the admitted cohort; each
subsequent exhibit reports its own base.

The first outcome is a binary indicator for reporting some digital
retention or use of remittance proceeds rather than withdrawing all
proceeds in cash. We also show the five original choices. Two choices
describe amounts on the same side of the halfway point in different
words. We retain both responses and do not use the five labels to
recover either an exact share or a strictly ranked cash-to-digital
scale. The indicator describes general disposition, not the amount
spent or the latest receipt channel. We report latest channel and
12-month international receipt frequency separately.

The second outcome is any reported domestic digital payment or transfer
in the previous 60 days. The denominator includes valid yes/no answers;
refusal is not coded as no. The number-of-operations question is
conditional on reporting domestic participation. Among affirmative
users we report the five original bins on the base of users giving a
substantive bin, with uncertainty shown separately. The bin wording
has shared endpoints, and the responses cannot recover transaction
counts. We report the six response states alongside the bins so that
the valid-bin denominator does not silently stand for all affirmative
users. Other questions keep their own routes and time references; for
example, the fee-visibility item refers to the last operation in a
90-day window, not the domestic outcome's 60-day window.

Three survey measures enter the working models. The first combines
reported phone access/use, internet-use frequency and disruptive data
or balance runout; some of
its content is close to the use outcomes. The second averages reported
ability to make a transfer and confidence in avoiding online fraud
and suspicious messages, not performance on a demonstrated task.
The third records two onboarding conditions:
proof of address and a phone line in the respondent's own name. It
does not measure actual eligibility for every provider, ownership of
an account, or formal receipt. Each measure requires its specified
items; missing components are not replaced with zeros or averaged out.
A three-item transfer/confidence alternative adds app installation
and has a different applicability rule. The supplement can compare
it to the two-item measure on the same cases as well as on the
separate pair-complete bases. Pearson associations do not validate
the measures or identify a mechanism.

## 4. Empirical approach

We describe both outcomes on their own valid-response bases. The
working conditional analysis uses a separate logit model for each
outcome. The core specifications include the three measures above,
age, grouped education and grouped recorded residence. Completed
secondary education and Bogotá are the respective reference
categories. The pooled residual residence group is a mix of recorded
responses, not a fourth city or a sampling cluster. The extensions
add grouped occupation, with employee or worker as reference, and
the original first-arrival calendar year. We do not reconstruct
tenure from that year. These model groupings do not replace the
descriptive response categories.

The five planned reporting rows distinguish the two core models, the
two extensions and the remittance core on the shared core base. That
last comparison asks what changes when the remittance model is
evaluated on the records also admissible to the domestic core model.
The accepted design checks that the shared base equals the domestic
core base before omitting a duplicate domestic fit. Complete, valid
inputs define eligibility for a model; they do not establish random
missingness. We report the intended and estimator sample sizes
separately and do not present an omitted observation as a new sample
exclusion.

For each core model we summarize the association with a measure as
the difference between two average fitted probabilities. We set that
measure to the lower and higher specified values in turn, keep the
other covariates at their observed values, and average over the same
estimation sample. The comparison is 0.50 to 0.75 for access and for
reported transfer/online-safety confidence, and 0.50 to 1.00 for the two onboarding
conditions. These are different increments, not interchangeable
units or interventions. Observed support at both endpoints does not
guarantee joint support for every combination used in standardization.

The six primary tests concern the corresponding coefficients in the
two core models. We report raw and Holm-adjusted p-values for that
fixed family. The contrast figure instead gives pointwise 95% delta
intervals, not simultaneous intervals. The robust covariance is a
working approximation conditional on independent records; it does
not correct for dependence through households or referrals. The
survey is cross-sectional, so the associations cannot distinguish
prior skills from skills or confidence gained through use, and
adjusting for observed covariates does not eliminate those or other
sources of confounding.

## 5. Reading the evidence for policy

A higher rate of digital use would not by itself establish lower costs
or better protection. A respondent may keep part of a transfer digitally
to make a payment and still rely on cash to pay for other goods. Charges
may be clear in one setting and hard to anticipate in another. The
survey can describe those reports on their respective bases. It
cannot measure the net cost of switching channels without comparable
information about prices, travel time and the alternatives available.

Likewise, an adverse experience, a complaint and its outcome are
different events. We do not treat a reported problem as verified
fraud, silence as satisfaction, or the absence of a complaint as
evidence that a remedy worked. The original qualitative accounts
and expert evidence can help explain where a respondent seeks help
and what formal avenues exist. They answer different questions:
accounts of experience can explain a mechanism worth investigating;
an expert description of an official channel does not establish
that any given respondent used it or was able to recover funds.

An implication for evaluation follows from these distinctions.
Providers and regulators can examine disclosure before confirmation
and access to recourse separately from transaction uptake. Whether
changes to those features improve welfare would require evidence on
realized fees and time costs, losses or recovery, or control over
spending, along with a credible comparison. Our survey cannot supply
that comparison. The paper can identify the questions that an
implementation or evaluation should address without ranking policy
options by unmeasured welfare gains.
